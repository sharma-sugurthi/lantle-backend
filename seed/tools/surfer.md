## What is Surfer?

Surfer is a content optimisation tool. You give it a target keyword and a location, it pulls the pages currently ranking for that term, analyses their length, headings, term usage and structure, and turns that into a live scoring editor. As you write or paste in a draft, the Content Score updates and a side panel shows which terms are underused, which are overused, and how your word count and heading count compare with the competition. The editor works in the browser and as a Google Docs and WordPress add on, so writers can optimise in place.

Around the editor Surfer has grown a set of adjacent features. Keyword Research clusters terms by topic and intent. Content Audit scores existing pages and lists what to fix. Surfer AI generates a full draft from the same data the editor uses, and a humanizer pass reworks obviously machine sounding text. Pricing is a monthly subscription based on how many editor documents you create, with add ons for AI drafts and larger teams.

## Where it shines

The editor is the best implementation of its kind. The scoring is transparent enough that writers understand why a term matters, and the guidance is granular without being prescriptive. Writers with no SEO training can produce a piece that competes on the page-level signals within an afternoon, and editors can set a target score as an acceptance criterion instead of arguing about keyword density.

It fits into existing workflows. Content teams that draft in Google Docs do not have to change anything. Agencies can share an editor link with a freelance writer who has no Surfer account, and the writer sees the same guidance. Publishing through the WordPress plugin closes the loop.

Content Audit is a practical way to work through a decaying blog. It ranks pages by potential, tells you which terms and internal links are missing, and estimates what fixing each page is worth. For sites with hundreds of old articles it turns a vague refresh project into a prioritised list.

The AI drafting feature is better than a generic prompt because it starts from the SERP analysis. Outlines match what is already ranking, and the draft arrives with the term coverage mostly done, so the editing task is adding expertise and removing filler rather than restructuring.

## Where it falls short

Surfer measures correlation with what ranks, not what makes a page good. Follow the score blindly and you get long, thorough, slightly bloated articles that hit every term and say nothing new. Search engines have been rewarding first hand experience and original information, and the score cannot see either. The best teams treat the score as a floor, not a target.

It is narrow. There is no backlink data, no rank tracking of any depth, no technical crawl and no paid search research. Surfer answers the question of how to write this page, not which pages to write or why traffic fell. Most teams run it alongside [Semrush](/tools/semrush/) for keyword discovery and site-wide reporting, and the two subscriptions together are not cheap.

The document based pricing is awkward. Teams that optimise many short pages burn through allowances quickly, and the AI drafting credits are a separate line item that is easy to overspend on. If the bottleneck is producing the copy itself rather than shaping it, [Jasper](/tools/jasper/) is the better fit and can pull Surfer guidance into its editor.

## Conclusion

For teams whose main job is publishing articles that rank, Surfer is the tool to buy. The editor makes on-page optimisation a repeatable step that any writer can follow, the audit feature gives a refresh programme real priorities, and the integrations mean nobody has to change how they write. Budget for a research suite alongside it, and keep a human in charge of what the article actually says.
