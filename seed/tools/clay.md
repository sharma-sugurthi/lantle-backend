## What is Clay?

Clay is a spreadsheet style workspace where each row is a company or a person and each column is an enrichment step. You start a table from a search, a CSV, a CRM segment or a webhook, then add columns that call data providers, scrape web pages, run AI prompts or trigger actions. Instead of subscribing to a dozen data vendors individually, you buy Clay credits and spend them across the providers Clay has integrated, with waterfall enrichment that tries one source after another until it finds a valid email or phone number.

The AI columns are where it gets interesting. Claygent, Clay's research agent, will visit a company's website, read its careers page or pull a LinkedIn profile and answer a question you define, such as whether the company sells to accountants or who owns finance operations. Formula columns, conditional runs and lookups let you branch logic within a table. Results push to HubSpot, Salesforce, outbound sequencers or a Google Sheet, and tables can run on a schedule so new signals are enriched and routed without anyone opening the tool.

## Where it shines

The flexibility is unmatched. Anyone who has stitched together Zapier, a data vendor, a scraping script and a spreadsheet to build a targeted list will recognise what Clay replaces. A single table can find accounts that just posted a specific job, identify the hiring manager, verify an email through three providers, write a first line that references the posting, and push the contact to a sequence, all with no code.

Waterfall enrichment measurably improves match rates. Because no single provider covers every segment, cascading through several sources raises the share of records with a valid email well above what one vendor delivers, and you only spend credits when a source returns a result.

The AI research columns turn qualitative signals into structured data. Questions that used to require an SDR reading websites for an afternoon, such as whether a company has a mobile app or which payment processor it uses, become a column that runs across thousands of rows.

The community and template library are strong. Public templates for common plays, an active Slack community and a growing agency ecosystem mean most workflows have been built by someone before.

## Where it falls short

Clay rewards people who think in tables and logic, and punishes those who do not. It is a builder's tool. A sales rep who wants a list this afternoon will find the interface overwhelming, and most teams need one person who owns Clay the way someone owns a data warehouse. If nobody on the team enjoys building this kind of system, [Apollo](/tools/apollo/) gets you a usable list with far less effort.

Credit consumption is hard to predict. A column that runs an AI model against every row, or a waterfall that hits five providers, burns credits quickly, and the cost of a table is not obvious until it has run. Teams regularly overspend in the first month before learning to run enrichments on filtered subsets and to test on a handful of rows first.

Clay is not an outreach tool. It has no sequencer, dialer or inbox of its own, so you still need a sending platform and a CRM. It does not replace [HubSpot Sales Hub](/tools/hubspot-sales-hub/) or another CRM as the system of record; it is the pipe feeding them.

## Conclusion

Clay is the most capable prospect research and enrichment tool available, and for a RevOps or growth person who likes building systems it is close to essential. The trade is complexity and cost discipline: it needs an owner, careful credit management and a separate CRM and sequencer around it. Teams without that owner should start with a simpler data provider and graduate to Clay when their plays outgrow it.
