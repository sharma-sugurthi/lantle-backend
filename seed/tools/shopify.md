## What is Shopify?

Shopify is a hosted commerce platform: you pay a monthly subscription, pick a theme, load products, and Shopify runs the storefront, checkout, payments and hosting. It has grown from a small business store builder into something that also serves enterprise brands through Shopify Plus, with the same admin underneath. Point of sale, wholesale through the B2B features, marketplaces and social channels all plug into one catalogue and one order list.

The AI layer arrives under two names. Shopify Magic is the set of generative features scattered through the admin: it drafts product descriptions, suggests email subject lines, writes replies in Shopify Inbox and generates backgrounds for product photos. Sidekick is the assistant that sits in the admin and answers questions about your own store, from why sales dipped last week to how to set up a discount, and it can carry out tasks such as building a segment or creating a report when asked. Both are included in the subscription rather than sold as add-ons.

## Where it shines

The checkout is the reason many brands pick Shopify and stay. Shop Pay stores customer details across every Shopify store, so returning shoppers finish in a couple of taps, and conversion on that path is consistently strong. You do not have to maintain it, tune it or worry about PCI scope, and improvements ship to every merchant at once.

The app ecosystem is the second big advantage. Almost any function you can think of, from subscriptions to returns portals to loyalty, exists as an app that installs in minutes. Theme customisation through the online store editor lets a non-developer rearrange sections and add app blocks without touching Liquid code. When you do need custom work, the developer community is large and the documentation is good.

Sidekick is more useful than most admin assistants because it has real context. Asking it to explain a drop in average order value returns an answer grounded in your data rather than a generic help article, and asking it to draft a discount produces the actual form filled in for review. Magic's product description drafts are decent starting points, particularly for long catalogues where writing every listing from scratch is not realistic.

Operationally, the platform is reliable. Flash sales that would bring down a self-hosted store tend to pass without incident, and multi-currency selling through Markets handles pricing, duties and localised domains from one admin.

## Where it falls short

Costs accumulate. The base subscription is reasonable, but payment processing fees, the extra transaction fee if you use a third-party gateway, and a stack of paid apps can push the monthly bill well above what the plan page suggests. Brands at scale often find themselves paying for several apps that each do one small thing.

The catalogue model has limits. Complex product configurations, heavy B2B pricing matrices and deep content-led sites can strain the standard data model, and workarounds through metafields and apps add maintenance. Merchants with very large, highly attributed catalogues sometimes outgrow the search and filtering that ship by default.

Native merchandising is basic. Shopify gives you collections and manual sorting, but personalised recommendations, smart bundles and cart upsells are mostly left to apps. Tools like [Rebuy](/tools/rebuy/) exist precisely to fill that gap. Likewise, the built-in inventory reports will not forecast demand or build purchase orders, which is where something like [Inventory Planner](/tools/inventory-planner/) takes over.

## Conclusion

Shopify remains the default choice for a hosted store, and the AI features now make the admin noticeably faster to work in rather than just adding a chatbot. The trade-off is a platform that is opinionated and can get expensive once you layer on apps. If you want to run a store rather than build one, and you accept that specialist functions will come from the ecosystem, it is the strongest option available.
