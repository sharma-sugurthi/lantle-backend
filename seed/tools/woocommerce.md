## What is WooCommerce?

WooCommerce is a free plugin that adds a shop to WordPress. Install it, run the setup wizard, and you have products, variations, a cart, checkout, order management, shipping zones and tax rules inside the same admin you already use for pages and posts. It is open source and maintained by Automattic, the company behind WordPress.com, and it powers a very large share of the online stores on the open web.

The business model is different from a hosted platform. The core plugin costs nothing. You pay for hosting, for a theme if you want a premium one, and for the extensions that add subscriptions, bookings, memberships, advanced shipping or marketplace feeds. WooPayments, the built-in payment option, earns Automattic a processing fee. If you would rather not manage a server, Woo also offers a hosted route through WordPress.com, but most stores still run on their own hosting.

## Where it shines

Ownership is the main reason to choose it. The database is yours, the code is yours, and there is no platform that can change its terms, remove an app or decide that your product category is no longer welcome. Exporting a store to another host is an afternoon of work rather than a migration project, and there is no revenue share on sales made through a third-party gateway.

Content and commerce live together. Because the store is WordPress, a long-form buying guide, a product page and a checkout are all first-class pages in one system. Brands that sell through editorial, SEO and email tend to prefer this to bolting a blog onto a store builder. The block editor and full site editing now handle most layout needs without code, and the newer block-based cart and checkout are quicker than the older shortcode versions.

The extension market is enormous. Between the official Woo marketplace and the wider WordPress plugin directory there is a solution for almost every requirement, often several, at prices that undercut hosted app stores. The REST API is complete, developer documentation is good, and the pool of freelancers who know it is the largest in ecommerce.

Core merchandising is better than people expect. Upsells and cross-sells are fields on every product, related products are automatic, and the built-in analytics screens cover revenue, products, categories, coupons and stock without a paid add-on.

## Where it falls short

You are the operations team. Hosting quality determines speed and uptime, plugin updates can conflict, and security patches are your responsibility. A store with thirty plugins from thirty authors is a maintenance burden that a hosted platform simply does not have, and a bad host will make even a small catalogue feel slow.

Checkout conversion depends on you tuning it. There is no shared wallet like Shop Pay that remembers customers across stores, and express payment buttons need WooPayments or a specific gateway extension to appear. Flash sale traffic is also your problem to provision for.

Costs are less visible than they look. Free core plus a good host, a handful of premium extensions with annual renewals, and developer time for updates can add up to a bill close to a mid-tier hosted plan. If you want to run a store rather than maintain one, [Shopify](/tools/shopify/) removes the whole infrastructure question, and [BigCommerce](/tools/bigcommerce/) ships more advanced catalogue and B2B features without extensions.

## Conclusion

WooCommerce is the best option when control matters more than convenience. Content-led brands, businesses with unusual requirements, and anyone wary of platform lock-in will find that the trade of some maintenance effort for complete ownership is worth it. Teams without technical help, or those who want checkout and hosting handled for them, should look at a hosted platform instead.
