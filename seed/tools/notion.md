## What is Notion?

Notion is a workspace where pages, wikis and databases live in the same tree. Every page is built from blocks: text, headings, toggles, embeds and, most importantly, database views. A database can be a table one moment and a kanban board, calendar or gallery the next, with the same rows underneath. Teams use this to run project trackers, lightweight CRMs, content calendars, meeting notes and company handbooks without leaving the app.

Notion AI sits on top of that structure. It can draft or rewrite text inside any page, summarise long documents, autofill database properties from the content of each row, and answer questions across the whole workspace. That last piece, workspace Q&A, also reaches into connected tools such as Slack and Google Drive, so a question about a project can pull from the spec, the meeting notes and the chat thread at once. Notion also ships a calendar app and an AI meeting notes feature that records and summarises calls directly into pages. The free plan is generous for individuals, while team administration and full AI usage sit on paid plans.

## Where it shines

The database model is the reason people stay. Relations and rollups let you connect a tasks database to a projects database to a clients database, then surface computed values such as open tasks per client without maintaining a spreadsheet on the side. Filters and sorts are saved per view, so a single source of truth can show an engineer their sprint, a manager the roadmap and a client a read-only status page.

Workspace Q&A becomes genuinely useful once a company has a few hundred pages. New hires ask where the expense policy lives or how a deployment works and get an answer with citations to the source pages, instead of interrupting someone in Slack. The autofill property feature is underrated: point it at a database of customer interviews and it can extract sentiment, key requests or a one-line summary for every row.

Templates, both first party and community made, cover most common setups, and the block editor makes writing long documents pleasant. Publishing a page to the web takes one click, which makes Notion a passable site builder for public docs and job boards.

## Where it falls short

Flexibility is also the main cost. Notion does not tell you how to structure anything, so workspaces built by enthusiastic early employees often turn into a sprawl of half maintained databases and duplicate pages. Someone has to own the information architecture, and in many teams nobody does. AI answers are only as good as the pages people bothered to keep current, and stale content gets cited with the same confidence as fresh content.

Performance on large databases is a recurring complaint. Views with thousands of rows and several rollups load slowly, and the mobile apps lag behind the desktop experience for anything beyond reading and light editing. Offline support has improved but remains partial.

Notion is not a substitute for specialist tools. The meeting notes feature is convenient, but it lacks the CRM sync and cross-meeting search you get from [Fireflies](/tools/fireflies/), and it is not as cheap per seat as [Otter](/tools/otter/) for pure transcription. Similarly, Notion's database automations handle simple triggers inside the workspace, but connecting Notion to the rest of a SaaS stack still means reaching for [Zapier](/tools/zapier/).

## Conclusion

Notion earns the top spot in this category because it collapses several tools into one, and the AI layer now makes that consolidated knowledge searchable rather than merely stored. It rewards teams that invest a little discipline in structure. If your team wants a tightly opinionated project manager or a document editor with strong offline behaviour, look elsewhere. For everyone else, Notion is the default choice for a company wiki that also does real work.
