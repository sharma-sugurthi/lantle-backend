## What is Cal.com?

Cal.com is the open source alternative to Calendly. The hosted version works the way you would expect: connect Google, Outlook or Apple calendars, create event types with durations, buffers and availability windows, and send a link. The invitee picks a slot in their own time zone and the meeting lands in both calendars with a Cal Video, Zoom, Google Meet or Teams link attached. The whole application is also published under an open licence, so you can run it on your own infrastructure and read every line of what it does with your calendar data.

The team features have caught up with the market over the last couple of years. Round robin event types distribute bookings across a group with weighting and priority, collective events find a slot that works for everyone, and routing forms send a visitor to the right person based on their answers. Workflows handle reminders and follow-ups by email or text. Beyond that, Cal.com has an app store of integrations, a full API, and a platform product with embeddable components so a SaaS company can drop scheduling into its own interface rather than sending users to an external booking page.

## Where it shines

The free plan for individuals is generous. Unlimited event types, unlimited bookings, multiple calendar connections and most of the workflow features are included without paying, which is a wider free tier than the incumbent offers. A consultant or freelancer can live on it indefinitely.

Self-hosting is the real differentiator. Regulated companies, agencies serving privacy-sensitive clients and anyone in a jurisdiction with strict data residency rules can run the whole stack themselves. The hosted product carries the usual compliance attestations, but for some buyers a booking page that never leaves their own servers is the only acceptable answer, and Cal.com is the credible option for that.

For product teams the platform offering is strong. The embed options go beyond an iframe: there are component libraries and API endpoints that let you build a fully branded scheduling flow, manage users programmatically and keep the invitee inside your application. Marketplaces, coaching platforms and telehealth products use it this way.

Development pace is fast and public. Feature requests, bugs and roadmap discussions happen on GitHub, and the pace of shipped changes is visible to anyone evaluating whether the product is going to keep up.

## Where it falls short

Fast development has a cost. Cal.com changes often, and users regularly run into rough edges: settings that moved, an integration that behaved differently after an update, or a booking page that renders oddly in a particular client. It is more stable than it was, but [Calendly](/tools/calendly/) still has the edge on polish and predictability, and for a sales team where every broken link is a lost meeting that matters.

Self-hosting is not free in practice. You need someone to run the database, keep up with releases and manage the calendar OAuth credentials, and some features in the hosted version are gated behind a paid licence key even on your own server. Small teams without engineering support should treat self-hosting as a project, not a checkbox.

The CRM routing depth is thinner than Calendly's. Routing forms work, but the lookup of existing accounts and assignment to an owner is less mature, and admin controls for very large deployments are still developing. Teams that want the calendar itself to plan their day rather than just accept bookings should look at [Motion](/tools/motion/), which solves a different problem.

## Conclusion

Cal.com is the right choice when control matters more than polish: you want to self-host, you want to embed scheduling in your own product, or you object to paying per seat for features that are free here. Individuals get an excellent free tool. Sales and recruiting teams that depend on flawless routing and a well-worn invitee experience should still weigh Calendly carefully before switching.
